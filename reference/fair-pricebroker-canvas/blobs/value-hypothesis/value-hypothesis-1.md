# Value hypothesis

## The problem

Third-party recruiters waste time and commission on candidates who will never accept what the client will pay. The current fix — a verbal salary check on the first call — works but runs into a structural problem: candidates are actively coached never to name a number first, so the check depends on a standoff that neither side wants to break.

## The solution

fairprice.broker is a lightweight, two-sided salary overlap tool. The recruiter submits the employer's budget range. The candidate answers Van Westendorp-style questions where naming a lower figure is genuinely in their interest — it signals openness to the right opportunity and increases their chances of a match. Neither side sees the other's raw numbers. Both see a two-dimensional output:

1. **Overlap level** — in range, stretch, or no overlap
2. **What to do next** — whether non-salary factors (culture, purpose, equity, flexibility) need to be meaningfully in play to bridge the gap

## Why it beats the status quo

- Breaks the "who names first" standoff without making either party feel exposed
- Candidate incentives are aligned: a lower figure earns more opportunities, not fewer
- The broker (recruiter) sees both sets of numbers and can act on the full picture
- Faster and more structured than a phone screen; no scheduling required

## Who it's for

**Beachhead:** Third-party / agency / contingency recruiters. They only earn on placement, so every misaligned candidate is a direct cost. Their ATS tools (Bullhorn and equivalents) offer no native comp alignment.

**Second wave:** Startups hiring directly — same pain, smaller volume, price-sensitive.

## Why now

Adversarial Van Westendorp — applying pricing-sensitivity methodology to a two-sided salary negotiation — has no known precedent in commercial tools or academic literature. Build cost is near-zero. The revenue threshold to justify the project is low. This is a launch and customer acquisition problem, not a market size problem.
